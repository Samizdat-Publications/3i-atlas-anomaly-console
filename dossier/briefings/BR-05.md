## The briefing

**Short answer:** Less than either side usually claims. Every dataset here has a bias that can manufacture a trend on its own, and knowing which bias applies to which question is most of the analysis.

### A catalog is not a census

The CNEOS fireball record contains 1,069 rows going back to 1988, and the count per year steps sharply upward in 1994 and again around 2000 before going flat for 26 years. Those early years are not quiet years; they are years with almost no reporting, because the satellite record these detections come from begins in the mid-1990s. CNEOS says as much itself: not all fireballs are reported, and the agency does not independently verify the events. A record whose own publisher says it is incomplete cannot be read as a measurement of the sky.

### Detection floors decide arguments

CNEOS lists no event below 0.048 kilotonnes, and only 6 of its 1,069 rows fall under 0.073. That floor is where a great many arguments quietly live. A skeptic cannot cite sensor silence about events the sensors cannot detect; a proponent cannot cite confirmation from an instrument that never saw the events in question. The gap between what eyewitnesses can see and what sensors can measure is roughly 0.001 to 0.05 kilotonnes wide, and it is a property of the hardware rather than a debating position.

### Growing instruments look like changing skies

Three datasets here grew enormously over their own record: US sensor coverage, the eyewitness reporting audience, and a camera network that went from 73 stations to over 1,300. Every one of them produces a rising raw count that has nothing to do with the sky. The fix is always the same — divide by something that grew with it. Bright events as a share of all events. Events above an energy a detection change cannot inflate. Meteors per detection rather than meteors. If a trend survives that division it is worth discussing; if it does not, what grew was the instrument.

### F-03 — Are fireballs increasing? Four instruments, and the one that sees daylight

- **Object:** CNEOS fireball register
- **Date:** 2026-08-15
- **Verification:** `CONFIRMED`

**OBSERVATION — what was measured**

Four datasets can speak to this, and no two of them measure the same thing. The American Meteor Society logs EYEWITNESS REPORTS — its unit is people, not photons, so it rises when cameras, phones and audiences do. NASA/JPL CNEOS logs US Government SENSOR DETECTIONS with an energy in kilotons, immune to how many people were watching, but with a hard floor: the smallest of its 1,069 rows is 0.048 kt and only 6 fall below 0.073 kt. The Global Meteor Network runs cameras that compute an ABSOLUTE MAGNITUDE for every meteor, with no human deciding whether to file anything — but it grew from 73 stations to 1,327 across this period, is blind in daylight, and is concentrated over Europe and North America. And NASA's Geostationary Lightning Mapper pipeline logs BOLIDE DETECTIONS from orbit — 11410 of them between 2017-07-23 and 2026-08-15 — assigning each an ordinal brightness. It was added to this case to answer the objection the other three cannot: staring down from geostationary orbit, IT SEES DAYLIGHT. 47.3% of its detections are daytime (5396 day against 5364 night), which is the half of the population a ground camera network never sees. Every one of the four has a bias that can manufacture a trend on its own. Only the statistics that normalise those biases away are worth comparing across years.

**CLAIM — the anomaly / technosignature reading**

The claim, made most carefully by the Angry Astronaut channel in August 2026, is that 2026 delivered a genuine spike in bright fireballs — and, crucially, that it is not merely an artifact of more people looking up. As put on 2026-08-23: in the first quarter alone, events drawing 50 or more reports more than doubled the recent average and events with 100-plus reports roughly doubled, so the larger, brighter events themselves increased. That is a real, checkable proposition, and it already anticipates the obvious objection rather than ignoring it. Checked against AMS itself, the figures are ACCURATE. Q1 2026 logged 25 events in the 51-99 report band against a 2021-2025 mean of 11.8, and 16 events above 100 reports against a mean of 8.8 — 41 high-report events where the recent average is 20.6. Even as a share of all events, which corrects for the dataset simply getting bigger, high-report events ran 1.715% of the total against a 1.067% norm: 1.61 times the usual proportion, the highest in the twenty years AMS publishes.

> The signal wasn't just more people looking up. The larger, brighter events themselves increased.
>
> — ANGRY ASTRONAUT · YOUTUBE 2026-08-23 · TRANSCRIBED FROM AUTO-CAPTIONS, NOT A PUBLISHED TEXT

**COUNTER-READING — the mainstream explanation**

The AMS figures are right and still cannot carry the conclusion, because AMS counts reporters. A growing audience does two things at once: it surfaces more events AND it gives each event more reports, pushing events UPWARD through the bins. An event drawing 20 reports in 2022 draws 45 in 2026 and leaves the 11-25 band. So the flat middle bin is not evidence against the bias — it is the bias. The diagnostic is whether well-witnessed events outgrew the dataset as a whole, and they did not: in Q1 2026 all events ran 1.23 times the 2021-2025 mean while events with 11 or more reports ran 1.21 times it. Same population, shifted rightward. CNEOS, which cannot be moved by audience at all, shows nothing: 18 events from 1 January to 15 August against a 2016-2025 range of 18 to 31, with one event at or above 1 kilotonne. But CNEOS cannot settle it either, because essentially every event in this argument sits below its floor. The tiebreaker is the Global Meteor Network, which measures brightness directly. Normalised as bright meteors (absolute magnitude -4 or brighter) per detection — a ratio that holds however many cameras are running — Q1 2026 gives 0.286% against a 2021-2025 mean of 0.264%, a factor of 1.08 against year-to-year scatter running 0.131% to 0.440%. Across January to July, 0.275% against 0.256%. Flat, at both thresholds GMN records. The obvious confound is ruled out rather than assumed: had cameras grown more sensitive, fainter detections would swell the denominator and hide a real rise, but the median absolute magnitude holds between -0.09 and +0.05 from 2021 to 2026. THE DAYLIGHT OBJECTION HAS NOW BEEN ANSWERED BY A FOURTH INSTRUMENT, and it is the reason this case was rewritten. GMN sees nothing in daylight, and several of 2026's most prominent events were daytime — the Lake Erie bolide at 8:57 a.m. Eastern drew 222 reports across 16 states. NASA's GLM pipeline has no such blind spot: 47.3% of its 11410 detections are daytime. Over the same like-for-like window, 1 January to 15 August, its bright-category share runs 3.00% in 2026 against a 2021-2025 mean of 2.80% — a factor of 1.07, against year-to-year scatter of 2.03% to 4.38%. GMN's figure for its comparable window was a factor of 1.07. Two instruments built on different physics, one of them blind to half the sky's clock, agreeing to two decimal places is the strongest statement this register can make about the question. THE GLM CATALOG HAS ITS OWN TRAP AND IT WAS WALKED INTO DELIBERATELY: on 2025-03-06 its pipeline began auto-publishing detections that previously required human review, so 2025-26 contain a class of record earlier years lack. Filtering on the confidence field does not fix that — review lags, so it filters on how recent an event is. Running the statistic both ways does: restricted to human-published events only, the share is 3.10% against 2.88%, a factor of 1.08. The share survives the regime change; the bright COUNT does not, which is why the count is not quoted here. GLM also drifts in sensitivity the way GMN does not — its faint-category share climbs from 29.9% in 2019 to 71.4% in 2025 — so it corroborates GMN rather than standing on its own. Its coverage is the GOES field of view, the Americas and neighbouring oceans, not global. GMN's coverage is regional too, and its brightest bin is small and noisy. So this is not a refutation, and the central observation stands exactly as stated. What has moved is the burden: every measure that corrects for instrument growth is flat, and the cameras built to measure brightness did not record the change.

### F-01 — IM1 — the half-metre bolide that hit at 44.8 km/s

- **Object:** CNEOS fireball register
- **Date:** 2014-01-08
- **Verification:** `CONFIRMED`

**OBSERVATION — what was measured**

At 17:05:33 UTC, US Government sensors logged a fireball over the Bismarck Sea north of Manus Island, Papua New Guinea. CNEOS lists the row at 1.2°S 147.1°E, peak brightness at 18.7 km altitude, a pre-entry speed of 44.8 km/s and a total impact energy of 0.13 kt TNT — a body roughly half a metre across. As the catalog stood at the 2026-08-22 pull it was the fifth-fastest of the 360 entries that report a speed at all. Two things about that row matter more than the numbers themselves: CNEOS publishes no uncertainties on any field, and the sensors that produced it are classified, so the measurement cannot be independently audited. The published papers arguing this case quote a box centre of 1.3°S 147.6°E; the catalog now lists 1.2°S 147.1°E, having been revised in the interim.

**CLAIM — the anomaly / technosignature reading**

Siraj & Loeb reconstructed the velocity vector as hyperbolic — gravitationally unbound from the Sun at 99.999% confidence — with an asymptotic speed of 42.1 ± 5.5 km/s and a striking 58 ± 6 km/s relative to the Local Standard of Rest, which would place its origin outside the thin disk of ordinary nearby stars. In April 2022 the US Space Command chief scientist reviewed the classified data and signed a memo stating that the velocity estimate reported to NASA was sufficiently accurate to indicate an interstellar trajectory. Loeb adds two further arguments: IM1's inferred material strength ranks first out of 273 CNEOS fireballs, and it fragmented unusually low for a chondrite at that speed. In June 2023 his Galileo Project ran a magnetic-sled expedition over the CNEOS box and recovered hundreds of sub-millimetre spherules whose beryllium-lanthanum-uranium enrichment he reports as unlike any solar-system material.

> Its high LSR speed implies a possible origin from the deep interior of a planetary system or a star in the thick disk of the Milky Way galaxy.
>
> — WITH A. SIRAJ · ASTROPHYS. J. 939:53 (2022), ABSTRACT

**COUNTER-READING — the mainstream explanation**

The critique attacks the one number everything rests on. Brown & Borovička (ApJ 953, 2023) compared USG fireball velocities against events with independent optical or infrasound data and found 10-15 km/s uncertainties at high speeds and radiant errors above ten degrees; their conclusion is that the simplest reading of IM1 is a substantially overestimated speed — and at a lower speed the orbit is bound and ordinary. That also dissolves the strength argument, because the ablation fits that make IM1 look ultra-strong only do so at the high assumed speed; the two 'anomalies' are one measurement, not two. Hajduková et al. (A&A 691, A8, 2024) went further: with no uncertainties published, nothing in the CNEOS data can confirm or reject an interstellar origin for any nominally hyperbolic entry. On the spherules, Gallardo (2023) matched the Be-La-U enrichment pattern to coal fly ash, a common shipping-lane contaminant, and Desch and colleagues argued a half-metre body arriving at 45 km/s would have almost entirely vaporised. In 2024 a team led by Benjamin Fernando reported that the seismic pulse used to pick the search site fitted a truck driving past the Manus Island seismometer, and placed the fireball more than 150 km from where the sled ran. Loeb answered that the expedition was guided by the 11-km CNEOS satellite box rather than the seismometer, and that Fernando's own infrasound ellipse (186 by 388 km) is large enough to contain it. The most sympathetic independent verdict is Socas-Navarro (2024), who redid the statistics and put the probability of an interstellar origin at 94.1% — significant, but short of the 3-sigma a discovery claim needs.
