### Why this one is different

Most claims in this register are about a single object or a single dataset. The nuclear connection is neither. It starts in December 1948, when aircrews over New Mexico reported brilliant green objects flying too slowly and too flatly to be meteors, and the Air Force put Dr Lincoln LaPaz, the meteoriticist who had built America's first serious fireball-recovery programme, onto the problem. He concluded they were not ordinary meteors and that they appeared preferentially near Los Alamos, Sandia and Kirtland. Everything since has that shape: the March 1967 Malmstrom episode, Rendlesham beside the Bentwaters weapons store, the interviews Robert Hastings collected from former missile officers. It is a large, specific, first-hand evidentiary base gathered from people with clearances and careers to lose, and it deserves to be taken at full strength rather than waved at.

### F-05 — Fireballs and volcanoes: testing the proximity claim

- **Object:** CNEOS fireball register
- **Date:** 2026-05-25
- **Verification:** `CONFIRMED`

**OBSERVATION — what was measured**

This is the one claim in the register that could be settled by arithmetic rather than argument, because both sides of it are already on file: 883 CNEOS events with coordinates, and 1,608 volcano positions published by NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information. The test asks whether fireballs fall closer to volcanoes than chance allows, using a Monte Carlo with 1,000 trials per null model. Choosing the null is the whole test. Scattering random points over the globe would be wrong, because volcanoes sit on land and cluster in arcs, so any land or latitude bias in the detections would fake an association. Two nulls are used instead, both preserving the detections' own latitude distribution: a ROTATION null applies one random longitude offset to every event at once, keeping the event set's internal clustering perfectly intact while destroying only its alignment with volcano longitudes; a SCATTER null gives each event a fresh uniform longitude. The result is the same either way. Within 100 km of a volcano: 23 events observed against 32.8 expected by chance. Within 200 km: 78 against 82.3. Within 500 km: 260 against 250.6. Median distance to the nearest volcano: 838 km observed against 854 km by chance. Not one of those is significant, and the closest bin, the one the claim is actually about, sits BELOW chance.

**CLAIM — the anomaly / technosignature reading**

The claim is that bright fireballs keep coming down at volcanoes, too often to be coincidence, and that this is one of the strongest hints that some of them are directed rather than random. It has a long pedigree in UAP research, objects reported flying through eruption plumes, hovering over craters, and 2026 supplied vivid new examples. On 2026-05-25 a green fireball appeared to strike the flank of Mayon in the Philippines while cameras watched, with a bright object seeming to rise from the same spot afterwards; PHIVOLCS said a meteor burned up high in the atmosphere and the rising object was a satellite. Weeks later another meteor was filmed above an erupting volcano elsewhere in Asia, and lights were recorded near Popocatépetl in Mexico on 2026-07-15 and again on the 28th. As put on 2026-06-16: 'another meteor appears above an erupting volcano just a couple of weeks after the first encounter just like it, and also in Asia.'

**COUNTER-READING — the mainstream explanation**

The sensor record shows no association at any distance, and the reason the visual record does is stated in the coverage itself without being noticed: 'Five Vulks cameras are rolling.' PHIVOLCS, the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology, keeps cameras trained on Mayon's sky continuously, because that is what a volcano observatory is for. Active volcanoes are among the very few places on Earth with fixed cameras pointed upward twenty-four hours a day, recording, archived, and watched by people who publish what they catch. A fireball over farmland is seen by whoever happened to look up. A fireball over a monitored volcano is filmed in high definition from several angles and posted the same day. That difference alone is enough to manufacture the entire pattern, and it is exactly the kind of selection effect CNEOS does not have: its detections come from satellite sensors that do not care whether anyone is filming. So the catalog is the right instrument for this question, and it answers no. The clustering claim fails the same way, 'three in the same place', with the median distance between an event and its nearest neighbour at 353 km against 357 km by chance, and 59 events having another within 100 km against 46.7 expected (p=0.13). That figure is computed after removing five CNEOS rows that appear twice: identical position and the same clock time to the second, on dates a few days apart, the signature of a mistyped date rather than two airbursts. Left in, each pretends to be a repeat at 0 km and the catalog seems to cluster at p=0.006; an earlier version of this test hid them by accident. THE LIMIT THAT CUTS THE OTHER WAY IS THE SAME ONE AS EVER, AND IT IS SERIOUS: neither the Mayon event nor the 2026-06-14 fireball has a CNEOS row at all, because both sit below the catalog's 0.048 kt floor. The nearest CNEOS row to Mayon in the entire record is 312 km away and from 2015. So this test says that the events big enough for satellites to see show no volcano preference; it cannot directly test the smaller events that the cameras actually caught. What it does establish is that the association does not exist in the one dataset immune to the camera effect, which is where a real physical association should have shown up first.
